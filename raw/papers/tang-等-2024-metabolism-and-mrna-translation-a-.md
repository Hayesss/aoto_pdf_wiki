Metabolism and translation: a tango in cancer plasticity
Tumor growth is critically dependent on protein synthesis, which itself requires significant energy,
diverse molecular building blocks, and ribosomal activity. Metabolic signals play a pivotal role in
regulating protein synthesis by shaping the allocation of cellular resources in response to varying
nutritional environments. The balance between the costs of protein biosynthesis and nutrient
availability creates an adaptive equilibrium that can influence the rate of mRNA translation and,
consequently, cellular proliferation or viability. Within this context, cancer cell plasticity, character-
ized by the dynamic ability to alter cell fate through translational reprogramming and metabolic
adaptations, enables tumor cells to sustain mRNA translation, to promote their growth, develop
therapeutic resistance, and adapt to the TME. For instance, nutrient scarcity within the TME –

Cancer cell metabolism fine-tunes mRNA translation
Metabolic cues directly control mRNA translation, allowing cells to swiftly adapt to fluctuating nu-
trient environments through the integrated stress response (ISR), an evolutionarily conserved sig-
naling network [10]. ISR-associated stress-responsive kinases – including protein kinase R-like
endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2), protein kinase
R (PKR), and heme-regulated inhibitor (HRI) – converge on the phosphorylation of eukaryotic ini-
tiation factor 2α (eIF2α). This reduces the availability of the eIF2-GTP-tRNA complex, leading to a
decrease in global protein synthesis [11]. In response to metabolic stress, the ISR-eIF2α axis
modulates protein homeostasis by reprogramming mRNA translation via cis-acting elements,
such as upstream open reading frames (uORFs). The mRNAs with uORFs in their 5′ untranslated
regions (UTRs) are preferentially translated during stress. Phosphorylation of eIF2α enables ribo-
somes to bypass uORFs, promoting the translation of key stress-responsive proteins such as ac-
tivating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) [12](Figure 1). ATF4 activates adaptive pathways that help
cells survive nutrient scarcity. Importantly, reducing ATF4 production through RNA silencing or in-
hibition of the ISR enhances the antitumoral effects of mitoribosome-targeting treatments in mel-
anoma, highlighting ATF4 as a potential therapeutic target [13]. Similarly, glutamine deprivation in
melanoma triggers invasiveness and therapy resistance via translation reprogramming driven by
ATF4 activation in response to inhibition of translation initiation factor eIF2B [14]. Metabolic alter-
ations also impact translation elongation. For instance, eukaryotic elongation factor 2 kinase
(eEF2K) is activated by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) during nutrient deprivation, leading
to decreased translation elongation. Modulation of eEF2K is crucial for the survival of cancer cells
under nutrient stress, with high eEF2K expression linked to poor prognosis in medulloblastoma
and glioblastoma multiforme [15]. Although AMPK was thought to activate eEF2K through direct
phosphorylation at serine 398 in normal cells, such as cardiomyocytes [16], pronounced differ-
ences in eEF2K activation upon nutrient deprivation has been observed between normal and
transformed cells. In transformed cells, eEF2K activation is markedly reduced. While both normal
and tumor cells experience reduced translation activity, impaired regulation of eEF2 in trans-
formed cells allows continued elongation on mRNAs, promoting their adaptation to starvation
conditions.
