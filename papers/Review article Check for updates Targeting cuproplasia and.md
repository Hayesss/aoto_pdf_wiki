## 摘要

Copper, an essential trace element that exists in oxidized and reduced forms, has pivotal roles in a variety of biological processes, including redox chemistry, enzymatic reactions, mitochondrial respiration, iron metabolism, autophagy and immune modulation; maintaining copper homeostasis is crucial as both its deficiency and its excess are deleterious. Dysregulated copper metabolism has a dual role in tumorigenesis and cancer therapy. Specifically, cuproplasia describes copper-dependent cell growth and proliferation, including hyperplasia, metaplasia and neoplasia, whereas cuproptosis refers to a mitochondrial pathway of cell death triggered by excessive copper exposure and subsequent proteotoxic stress (although complex interactions between cuproptosis and other cell death mechanisms, such as ferroptosis, are likely and remain enigmatic). In this Review, we summarize advances in our understanding of copper metabolism, the molecular machineries underlying cuproplasia and cuproptosis, and their potential targeting for cancer therapy. These new findings advance the rapidly expanding field of translational cancer research focused on metal compounds.



## 背景与目的

Essential trace elements, required by the human body in small amounts, have pivotal roles in a diverse range of biological processes. Copper, for example, can exist in Cu(II) and Cu(I) oxidation states, with Cu(I) being predominant in the reducing environment of the cytoplasm, and this interconversion between Cu(II) and Cu(I) imparts copper with intrinsic redox properties that are crucial for numerous physiological processes. Indeed, copper is a cofactor for key enzymes (known as cuproenzymes), including cytochrome c oxidase (also known as complex IV), which is required for cellular respiration and energy production as a component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain<sup>1</sup>. Apart from its enzymatic role, copper also regulates cell growth and function. Insufficient levels of copper lead to growth inhibition, whereas excessive copper exposure causes oxidative stress, cell death and tissue damage<sup>2</sup>. An imbalance or deficiency in copper compromises health, particularly in certain genetic disorders such as Menkes disease<sup>3</sup> and Wilson disease<sup>4</sup> (which are caused by germline mutations in the copper-transporting ATPase-encoding genes ATP7A and ATP7B, respectively). Dysfunction in copper homeostasis is also observed in conditions such as Alzheimer disease<sup>5</sup>, Parkinson disease<sup>6</sup>, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)<sup>7</sup>, diabetes mellitus<sup>8</sup>, obesity<sup>9</sup> and cancer<sup>10</sup>.

Copper concentrations are often elevated in tumour tissues and/or the blood in patients with various cancers, including but not limited to breast, lung, digestive tract, oral, thyroid, gallbladder, cervical, ovarian, kidney and prostate cancers<sup>11</sup>. The role of copper in cancer is complex, with both tumour-promoting and tumour-suppressive effects<sup>12,13</sup>. ## 讨论与结论

Targeting cuproplasia and cuproptosis in cancer constitutes a multifaceted and promising therapeutic approach. Copper-based therapies can operate through various mechanisms, including the emerging concept of 'metalloallostery', whereby dynamic copper binding at non-catalytic sites regulates protein activity. This expanded knowledge sheds new light on the contribution of copper to protein modification and cellular processes. Additionally, the development of novel small-molecule fluorescent probes for the monitoring of copper signals or to identify nutrient sensor molecules for copper in cells will enhance our mechanistic understanding of copper-related processes. Transgenic animal models, including mouse and rat models of Wilson disease180, can provide valuable tools to decipher the role of copper abnormalities in tumour development and progression.

Identifying molecular tumour subtypes, biomarkers (for example, LDH) and cuproenzymes (such as COX, SOD1, ULK1, ULK2, MEK1 and MEK2) linked to copper dependency as well as genetic alterations affecting response to copper-based therapies might open new avenues to personalized cancer treatments. Moreover, determining the specific threshold levels of copper required for cuproplasia and cuproptosis might be particularly useful in selecting appropriate copper-related therapies. Given that FDX1 is widely expressed in both cancer and non-cancer cells, designing cell-specific cuproptosis-induction approaches for cancer therapy is crucial to reducing the risk of adverse effects. Combining copper-based therapies, such as copper chelators or ionophores, with chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy has shown promise in preclinical and, in some cases, clinical studies. These synergistic approaches have the potential to enhance treatment outcomes and address drug resistance through the induction of multiple and different cell death pathways.
