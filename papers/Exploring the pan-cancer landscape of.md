## 背景与目的

Deregulation of gene expression is a hallmark of cancer. Cancer gene expression is controlled at multiple steps, from transcription, mRNA stability, and mRNA translation up to protein degradation and posttranslational modifications. It is a long-standing question of how these regulatory steps contribute to protein levels and cancer progression. Although many cancer studies have focused on mRNA abundance as a readout for gene expression, RNA levels typically explain only roughly half of the variance in cellular protein levels, indicating that posttranscriptional regulation is an equally important determinant of cancer cell behavior.1–6 However, to what extent different cancer types alter systems-wide posttranscriptional control and which cohorts of genes are under strong posttranscriptional regulation are poorly understood.

Tumor initiation and progression are driven by mutations in cancer-associated genes. Mutations in cancer driver genes can directly or indirectly modulate the different steps of the gene expression cascade and theoretically increase or decrease the role of posttranscriptional regulation in determining protein abundance in patients with cancer. Decades of cancer research efforts have investigated the role of cancer driver mutations in altering gene expression on a single-gene basis. Nevertheless, which cancer mutations particularly impact global posttranscriptional regulatory mechanisms across different cancer types is an ongoing question.

Proteogenomic efforts over the last decade, mainly driven by the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) initiative to perform mass spectrometry-based proteomics, enable the assessment of the role of posttranscriptional regulation. A proxy for the extent of posttranscriptional regulation is the correlation between mRNA and protein levels, which is mainly driven by mRNA translation rates and protein degradation (Figure 1B).


## 主要发现

#### mRNA-protein correlations across 14 proteogenomic human cancer datasets

To compare pan-cancer mRNA-protein correlations, we included 14 proteogenomic human cancer datasets that contained transcriptomic and protein-level quantifications \(Figure 1A; Figure S1A). We incorporated in our analysis glioblastoma, pediatric brain cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, prostate adenocarcinoma, uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma, ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma, stomach adenocarcinoma, and colorectal carcinoma1,8–18 \(Figure 1A). 11 out of 14 studies were conducted within the CPTAC, indicating reliability in cross-comparing the different cancer datasets (Figure S1A).

We can distinguish two main types of mRNA-protein correlations. First, we can correlate the variation of the same mRNA and protein levels across different patients with cancer, which investigates how a particular mRNA's concentration explains the resulting protein levels in different patients with cancer (genelevel analysis). Second, we can correlate all mRNA-protein pairs in an individual patient with cancer to determine how the overall mRNA differences in a patient reflect their corresponding protein abundance (sample-level analysis). For the two types of correlations, we found median pan-cancer Spearman correlations for gene-level and sample-level analyses of 0.41 and 0.42, respectively. As expected, gene-level correlations show a wider distribution than sample-level correlations \(Figures 1C and 1D).

We focused our subsequent analyses on gene-level correlations across different cancer types, as this type of analysis allows us to explore differences in posttranscriptional control between different cohorts of genes. We observed a considerable range of median Spearman correlations across different cancer types \(Figure 1E).
